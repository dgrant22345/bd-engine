How to find the hiring manager for a job posting

Postings rarely name the person who owns the hire. This guide gives a short sequence for working it out from public information, for both staffing recruiters and job seekers, and says what to do when the answer stays uncertain.

Published by BD Engine November 12, 2026 7 minute guide

Three people, three jobs

A posted role usually involves a hiring manager, who leads the team and makes the decision; a recruiter or talent acquisition partner, who runs the process; and sometimes a senior leader who approved the headcount. Knowing which one you are writing to changes what you ask.

  • Job seekers generally want the hiring manager for a conversation about the work, and the recruiter for questions about the process.
  • Staffing recruiters often need talent acquisition first, because many employers route all agency contact through that team.

1. Read the posting on the employer's own site

Use the employer's careers page, not a reposted copy. Look for:

  • A “reports to” line or a named team.
  • The department, product, or site the role belongs to.
  • The location, which narrows the list of possible managers.
  • A named recruiter or contact, which some postings include.

The title of the manager is usually one level above the role in the same function: a Senior Analyst in finance operations most likely reports to a Manager or Director of Finance Operations.

2. Check what the employer publishes

Leadership and team pages, press releases, engineering or company blogs, and conference talks often name the head of a function. Smaller employers list most managers. Larger ones list senior leaders, which at least tells you whose organisation the role sits in.

3. Search by employer and likely title

On LinkedIn, search the employer together with the title you inferred and the location. Then look at people in the role being hired; their profiles and public posts often show the team name and who leads it. A manager who has shared the opening or written that the team is hiring has answered the question for you.

Do this by hand. Automated collection of profile data breaks most platforms' terms, and a list built that way tends to be wrong in ways you cannot see.

4. Ask someone who works there

A person you already know at the employer can usually name the manager in one message, and can tell you whether an approach would be welcome. Ask for the name and the context first. An introduction is a larger favour and is better requested once you know it is the right person. The connections guide covers how to find who you know at a given employer.

5. Confirm before you write

Treat one indication as a guess and two independent ones as good enough: for example, a title that fits the reporting line and a public post about the opening. If you are still unsure, say so in the message. “I believe this role sits in your team; if not, could you point me to the right person?” is honest and easy to answer.

Use business contact routes. Write through LinkedIn or a work address the person or employer has published. Guessing address patterns and sending to every variation is unreliable and may breach email rules; the business development email guide summarises those for Canada and the United States.

When you cannot tell

Sometimes public information does not settle it. In that case write to the talent acquisition team or the recruiter named on the posting, refer to the specific role and date, and ask who the right contact is. That is a normal request, and it avoids sending a confident message to the wrong manager.
