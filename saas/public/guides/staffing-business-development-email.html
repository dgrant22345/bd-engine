How to write a first business development email to a hiring manager

A hiring manager decides in a few seconds whether a message from a recruiter is about them. This guide sets out a four-part structure that makes that decision easy, with a worked example, a short list of what to leave out, and the email rules worth checking before you send.

Published by BD Engine November 12, 2026 8 minute guide

What the reader is deciding

The person opening your email is asking three things: is this about my team, does the sender know anything, and what do they want from me. A message that answers all three in four or five sentences can be dealt with on the spot. One that opens with your agency's history cannot.

Nothing here guarantees a reply. Many employers fill every role internally or buy only from a preferred supplier list. The aim is narrower: make it easy for the right person to say yes, no, or not now.

Four parts, in this order

1. The evidence line

Say what you saw and when: the role, the team, and the date it was posted on the employer's own careers page. This is the reason you are writing today and not last month.

2. The relevance line

One fact about your work that bears on that role: a comparable placement, a candidate type you can reach, or a market you recruit in every week. Keep it to something you could prove if asked.

3. One question

Ask something the reader can answer in a line, such as whether the team uses outside recruiters for this kind of role, or who looks after agency suppliers. Do not ask for a meeting and a referral and a job description in the same message.

4. A plain sign-off

Your name, your firm, and how to reach you. If the person would prefer not to hear from you again, say that a one-word reply is enough, and honour it.

A worked example

This is an illustration with invented details. Replace every part with facts you have checked. It shows structure only: add the sender identification and unsubscribe wording that the rules in your country require, which are summarised at the end of this guide.

Subject: Senior Controls Engineer role, posted October 28

Hello Priya,

Your team's Senior Controls Engineer role has been on the Northstar Robotics careers page since October 28, alongside two other automation openings.

I recruit controls and automation engineers in southern Ontario and placed two at a packaging-equipment manufacturer this year.

Does your team work with outside recruiters for roles like this, or is it all handled in-house?

If this is not for you, a one-word reply is enough and I will not write again.

Sam Ortiz, Ortiz Technical Search
sam@example.com · 416-555-0100

Subject lines

Write the subject as a label for the message: the role title and one concrete detail. A reader should be able to tell what the email is about without opening it.

  • Use the role title as the employer wrote it.
  • Do not start a new message with “Re:” or “Fwd:”. It is misleading, and it is the kind of subject line anti-spam rules are written against.
  • Skip “quick question” and similar openers. They describe every unsolicited email.

What to leave out

  • Claims about their situation. You have seen a public posting. You do not know how urgent the hire is or how the internal search is going.
  • Fees and terms. They belong in a conversation the reader has agreed to have.
  • Attachments and candidate profiles. Unrequested résumés create work for the reader and questions about consent for you.
  • Praise for the company. It reads as filler and delays the point.
  • A second ask. One question per message.

Following up

A follow-up should carry new information: a further role posted by the same team, a posting that has now been open for several weeks, or a relevant candidate who has become available. “Just checking in” gives the reader nothing new to decide.

Set a date when you send the first message. One or two follow-ups spaced a week or more apart is a reasonable limit. If someone asks you to stop, stop, and record it so nobody else at your firm writes to them about the same thing.

Check the email rules before you send

Commercial email is regulated, including messages between businesses. This is a summary of official guidance, not legal advice, and other countries have their own rules.

  • Canada. Canada's anti-spam legislation requires consent, sender identification, and an unsubscribe mechanism in each commercial electronic message. The regulator's guidance describes implied consent where a business address has been conspicuously published without a statement refusing such messages and the message relates to the recipient's role. The sender has to be able to show both. See the CRTC guidance on implied consent.
  • United States. The Federal Trade Commission states that the CAN-SPAM Act makes no exception for business-to-business email. Its requirements include accurate header information, a subject line that reflects the content, a valid postal address, and a clear way to opt out. See the FTC compliance guide.
  • Both. Each source gives ten business days as the limit for acting on a request to stop.
