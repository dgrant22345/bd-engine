How to find companies that are hiring: a staffing BD workflow

A target list tells you who you would like to work with. Hiring signals tell you who has a reason to talk now. This guide sets out which signals are worth tracking, where to read them, and how to turn them into a short daily routine.

Published by BD Engine November 12, 2026 7 minute guide

Start from evidence, not from a list of logos

Most staffing business development starts with a static target list and a call script. The list says who you would like to work with. It says nothing about who has a reason to talk this week. A hiring signal is a dated, checkable fact that gives the conversation a subject.

The most reliable signals are the ones an employer publishes itself: the roles on its own careers board, when each was posted, and how long each has stayed open.

Four signals worth tracking

1. Several new roles in a short period

Three or more openings posted within a week or two usually means a funded plan rather than a single backfill. Note the count and the dates, not only the company name.

2. Roles concentrated in one function

Five engineering roles tell you more than five roles spread across five departments. Concentration points to one hiring manager with a real capacity problem, and it tells you which of your candidates are relevant.

3. A role that has stayed open

A posting that is still live after several weeks is evidence that the internal search has been slow. It is not proof that the employer wants agency help, so treat it as a question to ask, not a conclusion.

4. A talent or hiring contact you already know

A first-degree connection in talent acquisition, or a leader of the team that is hiring, changes a cold approach into a relevant note to someone who recognises your name.

A posting is not a requirement for an agency. Many employers fill every role internally or work only from a preferred supplier list. A signal earns a well-informed first message. It does not earn an assumption.

Read the employer's own job board

Aggregators repost roles late, merge duplicates, and keep closed roles visible. The employer's applicant tracking system is the source. Many public boards run on a small number of systems, including Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, SmartRecruiters, Workday, BambooHR, Workable, Jobvite, Recruitee, Personio, and Rippling, and each exposes current openings with posting dates.

Coverage is never complete. Some employers use systems that cannot be read reliably, and some post only a part of what they are hiring for. Record which of your targets you can read and which you cannot, so that silence from an unreadable board is not mistaken for no hiring.

A routine that fits in half an hour

  1. Keep the target list short. Fifty accounts you can describe beat five hundred you cannot.
  2. Refresh the boards you can read. Note new roles, the function they sit in, and any role that has aged.
  3. Rank by signal and relationship together. An account with fresh roles and someone you know comes first. Fresh roles with no contact come second. A contact with no current hiring is a relationship to maintain, not a pitch.
  4. Review ten to twenty-five accounts. Read the actual postings for the few at the top.
  5. Write to five to ten people. Refer to the specific role and date, say what you have placed that is relevant, and ask one question.
  6. Record what happened. Set a dated follow-up. A signal that was worth one message is usually worth a second.

What the first message should contain

  • The specific evidence: the role, the team, and when it was posted.
  • One relevant fact about your work: a comparable placement or a candidate type you can reach.
  • One question the reader can answer in a line.

Leave out claims you cannot support, such as how urgent the hire is or how the internal search is going. You have seen a public posting. Say that, and ask.
