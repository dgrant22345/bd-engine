### Why staffing teams should stop calling a large account list “coverage”

Staffing business development has no shortage of large numbers.

A database may contain 5,000 companies. A monitoring tool may claim hundreds
of thousands of jobs. A desk may have thousands of LinkedIn connections. All
of those numbers can be true while the operating question remains unanswered:
which target accounts deserve attention today, and why?

The missing concept is a denominator.

If a team says it monitors 500 companies, the number means very little until
we know how many companies belong in the target universe, how many have a
usable public career source, how many sources can be interpreted reliably,
how many openings fit the desk, and how many accounts have a credible path to
a conversation.

That distinction matters because the public ATS market is fragmented.

I analyzed the 704 employers marked verified in the MIT-licensed ResumeAI
State of ATS 2026 dataset and mapped each provider to one of three BD Engine
handling tiers: automatic public-role import, source tracking without automatic
import, or discovery/manual review. The source dataset says its provider
assignments were refreshed against live careers-portal apply hosts in July
2026; I used only rows marked verified and did not independently re-verify each
employer.

In that large-employer sample, 401 employers—57.0%—use providers for which BD
Engine has an automatic public-role import path. Another 130 employers, or
18.5%, use systems the product can identify and track but does not treat as
automatically importable. The remaining 173 employers, or 24.6%, still require
another source strategy, discovery, or manual review.

The practical lesson is not that 57% is universally good or bad. It is that a
single platform-wide percentage is not the coverage rate for any individual
staffing desk.

Target mix changes the result. Among the 12 largest industry cohorts in the
same dataset, automatic-import share ranges from 10% in Transportation to 66%
in Technology. A specialist technology recruiter and a transportation staffing
firm can use the same product and face very different source-review workloads.

That should change how staffing teams build a BD workflow.

First, define the target universe. Use a bounded list that reflects the desk,
geography, role family, and client hypothesis. Twenty-five well-chosen accounts
are more useful for a pilot than 5,000 unqualified companies.

Second, separate source states. A recognized public host, a known system that
requires tracking, an ordinary careers page, an invalid URL, and an unresolved
company are not equivalent. Keeping those states visible prevents a coverage
metric from improving merely because uncertainty was hidden.

Third, separate job availability from relevance. A public posting is evidence
of hiring, but it is not automatically a reason to contact the company. The
role still needs to fit the desk, be sufficiently current, and add something
useful to the conversation.

Fourth, add relationship context. A target account with six relevant roles and
a credible warm path is operationally different from an equally active company
with no known contact. This is where a large existing network becomes useful:
not as an audience for mass outreach, but as context for choosing the next
human-reviewed action.

Finally, measure the outcome that matters. The useful output is not more
messages or more scraped jobs. It is a short queue of explainable next actions:
research this account, ask for this introduction, prepare this draft, or follow
up on this date.

Coverage is therefore a chain, not a headline count:

1. target-universe coverage
2. usable-source coverage
3. current hiring-signal coverage
4. role-relevance coverage
5. relationship coverage
6. action coverage

A weak link should remain visible because it tells the team what to fix next.

The larger point is simple. Staffing business development does not need another
promise of “complete market coverage.” It needs a transparent operating model
that shows the denominator, the evidence, the gaps, and the next decision.

audit are available at the canonical link supplied with this submission.

“Coverage” is not the number of companies in a database.

### Post 2 — Industry variation

The same ATS product can look strong in one staffing vertical and weak in
another.

In the 12 largest industry cohorts of a 704-employer verified sample,
automatic-import share ranged from 10% in Transportation to 66% in Technology.

That is a 56-point spread before we even ask whether the jobs are recent,
relevant to the desk, or connected to someone in the recruiter's network.

This is why I do not want BD Engine to hide unresolved sources or claim one
universal coverage number.

The operating metric is:

usable sources in your target universe ÷ all companies in your target universe

Then add role relevance, relationship coverage, and a human-reviewed next
action.

### Post 3 — What unresolved should mean

An unresolved career source is not a product embarrassment.

It is a work queue.

The dangerous version of an ATS dashboard quietly drops the company and lets
the reported coverage number improve.

The useful version keeps the company visible and tells you what to do next:

- verify company identity
- find the official careers page
- identify the hosted ATS
- choose another trustworthy public source
- keep it manual when automation would overstate confidence

For staffing BD, uncertainty is operational information.

I built a free browser-based audit that separates recognized hosts, ordinary
pages needing discovery, and invalid entries. Shared links include only the
aggregate counts—not the pasted URLs.

### Post 4 — Seven-day pilot

If you want to test a hiring-signal workflow, do not start with 5,000 accounts.

Start with 25.

1. Define why every company belongs.
2. Collect the best public careers URL.
3. Audit which sources are recognized, trackable, or unresolved.
4. Map existing connections to those companies.
5. Rank current roles by desk relevance.
6. Review the top next actions.
7. Measure useful conversations—not message volume.
